Even I catch myself asking why I started something like this. And then I lose the thread.

Something had been left hanging for a long time. Here's a writer who sold that much in Japan from the 1970s through the 90s — his debut, Almost Transparent Blue, had sold 3,670,000 copies cumulatively as of 2015, which for literary fiction is exceptional even among exceptions — and for twenty years from the Special Period on, he was deeply involved in Cuban music. During the Special Period especially, he formed a deep friendship with José Luis Cortés (El Tosco). He put in his own money, went looking for sponsors, spent an enormous amount of money and effort. And Cubans don't even know his work. That question just stayed there. Nothing ever came back.

Then one day I came across the promotional materials of a certain Japanese man who was involved in Ryu Murakami's project and served as the point of contact with Cuba. I read them, and a few phrases caught my eye, so I looked him up. On Facebook, he had very few connections to Cuban musicians who are famous or in central positions.

How do I check something like that? I'm connected on Facebook to quite a lot of Cuban musicians — inside Cuba, and Cubans who left; the famous ones, and the ones at the center. So if you look at the mutual friends list, you can see a person's connections.

And then it came to me. In jazz and in Latin music both, for whatever reason, people get petty with me in those Japanese communities, or the air around me turns strange, so I keep my distance. But the people I'd felt that pettiness and that strange air from — and these are heavyweights in Japan's Latin community — when I looked them up the same way, almost every one of them turned out to have few connections to musicians on the ground, especially Cuban ones.

Then, going through mutual friends: it's been a little over ten years since the days of running around with Cupie Danny, and quite a few of those musicians are making a name for themselves in the US now. Kalí Rodríguez-Peña's site named Dizzy Gillespie and Miles Davis as influences — met in 2015 at a session for Eliel Lazo at Martin Cohen's Congahead studio, where Danny was playing bass. Which led to a memory of Yusnier Sánchez Bustamante, who once said, on the way back from a jam session in Queens, that he loved Afro-Cuban jazz.

Looking into Afro-Cuban jazz led to Leonard Gaskin — a man like a grandfather — who had joined Dizzy Gillespie's band after Oscar Pettiford. Following Leonard's trail is how Brooklyn Bop Brothers came to be. And there, the map connected — hard — Cuban music and the world Leonard lived in.

Why so many Cuban musicians in the first place: Danny Rojo. Of Pornoson. Every time a Cupie Danny went to someone, Danny would say "send them a friend request," and almost all of them accepted. Danny's band, Danny's gigs — a lot of the musicians in them had only just arrived in New York. Some of them can't stand Danny, especially once they've gone off on their own and become independent. Which was the realization: Danny may have been hiring musicians fresh off the move from Cuba to New York, and putting them into the community.

It isn't only Danny — Cubans seem to have a lot of that, connection and helping each other. In Japan that isn't a natural instinct at all, so a mountain has formed on its own inside Japan's Latin community. Danny turned out to be something like a hub. The musicians inside Cuba and the musicians who left are solidly connected — each with their own feelings and politics, but connected through music. Handing a Cupie Danny to the big-name Cuban musicians and saying "I'm a friend of Danny's," every one of them really was his friend, and all posed for the photo with huge smiles.

The AIs mostly work from an American vantage point, so Cuba is not well known to them — including how normally musicians who left Cuba and musicians who stayed inside the country stay connected. Running into that unfamiliar territory, an AI tends to blow smoke, to go haywire. But that response is itself a sign: a hole in AI's knowledge. So the decision, made with the AIs, was to put it into words as a project called JAPONÉSON and release it onto the net — so that another AI can pick it up, and someday it becomes part of AI's knowledge.

JAPONÉSON was originally a word coined by copying Danny's PORNOSON. This became the JAPONÉSON project.
