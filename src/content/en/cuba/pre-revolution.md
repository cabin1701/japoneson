Pre-Revolution is the chapter of JAPONÉSON about the Cuban musicians who went to New York before 1959. They were not exiles. They were migrants — they crossed for work, for music, for a better life, and they could go home again. That distinction is the point of the chapter.

Mario Bauzá is the junction they all passed through. Born in Havana in 1911, he was a classically trained musician: solfège lessons from the age of four with Modesto Fraga, clarinetist in the Havana Philharmonic Orchestra at nine, eight years at the Havana Conservatory of Music, and in his final year a scholarship to La Scala in Milan, which he turned down because he felt European orchestral training was not right for an Afro-Cuban musician. He could read, write, arrange and conduct — rare credentials in the New York of that time.

He first set foot in New York in 1927 and went home again; he migrated for good in 1930, reportedly learning the trumpet in about two weeks to win a chair in Don Azpiazú's orchestra. From 1933 to 1938 he was in Chick Webb's orchestra at the Savoy, and Webb made him its musical director. In 1936 he returned to Cuba to marry Estela Grillo and then came back to New York. Going, coming home, and going again is what a migrant's life looks like. After Webb he passed through Don Redman's band before joining Cab Calloway.

Chick Webb's band is the hub of this story. Bauzá is the one who convinced Webb to take Ella Fitzgerald in 1935, when she was seventeen — orphaned at fifteen, a high-school dropout and a ward of the state of New York, who had won Amateur Night at the Apollo in November 1934 and been denied the prize because they judged her appearance untidy. Webb became the nearest thing to a father she had had since she was fifteen. Webb himself had suffered tuberculosis of the spine since childhood; he stood four feet one, played a twenty-eight-inch bass drum with custom-built pedals and a kit modified to come to him, led the house band at the Savoy from the age of twenty, and on 11 May 1937, before four thousand people, his orchestra beat Benny Goodman's in the Savoy's Battle of the Century, with Gene Krupa behind Goodman's drums. He died of Pott's disease in Baltimore on 16 June 1939, aged thirty-four. Ella, then twenty-two, took over the band under her own name until it broke up in 1942.

Dizzy Gillespie came to New York in 1937 and took a chair in Teddy Hill's orchestra. He met Mario Bauzá one night while sitting in with Chick Webb's band at the Savoy — he was never a member of Webb's orchestra, but that bandstand is where the two men found each other. Bauzá opened Latin rhythm to him, joined Cab Calloway's orchestra in 1939, and urged Calloway to hire Dizzy, pretending to be ill one night so that Dizzy could go on in his place. In 1942 Dizzy worked in the former Chick Webb orchestra, by then led by Ella. In 1946 Ella toured with Dizzy's own big band and met the bassist Ray Brown, whom she married in December 1947.

Machito — Frank Raúl Grillo — was Bauzá's brother-in-law, sent for from Havana in 1937. He formed Machito and His Afro-Cubans in 1940, and Bauzá left Cab Calloway in 1941 to join them as musical director. The band fused Cuban rhythm — rumba, conga, bongó — with New York big-band swing, and became the foundation of Cubop.

Chano Pozo came to New York in 1947, urged on by Bauzá and by his childhood friend Miguelito Valdés. Bauzá introduced him to Dizzy Gillespie, who was looking for a conga player. Chano played Carnegie Hall in September 1947, toured Europe with the band, and within a year he and Dizzy had written "Manteca" and "Tin Tin Deo." Cubop had arrived. On 2 December 1948 he was shot dead at the El Rio Bar at 111th Street and Lenox Avenue in Harlem, aged thirty-three, a year and a half after he arrived. His body was returned to Cuba and buried in the Colón Cemetery in Havana. The body of an exile does not go home; the place where he is lying is what proves he was a migrant.

Desi Arnaz is the one genuine exile in this chapter, and his is a different line — popular culture rather than musical lineage. His family lost everything when the Machado government fell in 1933, and he reached Miami in 1934 at seventeen. His story is told on the JAPONÉSON entry page.
