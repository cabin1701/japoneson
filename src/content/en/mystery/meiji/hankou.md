### 1. How well known is the Hankow Concession?

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** Vega, what about the Hankow Concession? How well known is it among Japanese people today?

</div>

**G-Vega:** To get straight to the point: **in contemporary Japan, the fact that Japan had its own concession in Hankow (Hankou) is virtually unknown.** (Opportunities to hear about it in formal education or mainstream media are extremely limited.)

It only ever registers with a very narrow group—history buffs, specialists in modern Japanese and Sino-Japanese history, or those exploring modern Chinese literature (such as Lu Xun or the Battle of Wuhan).

Behind this lies several major historical blind spots.


#### Why is it so little known?

* **The overwhelming dominance of the "Concession = Shanghai" image**
In Japanese textbooks and general media, the Shanghai International Settlement and the French Concession take center stage. Narratives overwhelmingly revolve around the Opium Wars and the glamorous modern architecture of the Bund in "Old Shanghai" (the Wicked City). The fact that miles up the Yangtze River into the inland strategic hub of Hankow, concessions for Britain, France, Russia, Germany, and **Japan's own Exclusive Concession** stood side by side is brushed past with barely a mention of the place name in standard education.


* **The disappearance of the city name "Hankow" (Integration into Wuhan)**
The historic "Three Towns of Wuhan" (Hankow, Wuchang, and Hanyang) were briefly unified in 1927, and after further reorganization, became the modern metropolis of "Wuhan City" in 1949. Partly because the city’s name changed, everyday recognition of the word "Hankow" itself remains quite limited among people in modern Japan.

* **How it is treated in history textbooks**
What typically appears in standard textbooks are isolated historical flashpoints: the 1911 "Xinhai Revolution (Wuchang Uprising)" and the 1938 "Battle of Wuhan" during the Second Sino-Japanese War. Because it is taught merely as a series of battlefield "dots," the rich daily and economic reality is almost completely lost—the fact that for nearly half a century (1898–1943), trading houses (Mitsui, Mitsubishi), the Yokohama Specie Bank, oil extraction refineries, traditional Japanese restaurants, and Japanese settlement schools operated here, forming a thriving, extraterritorial Japanese town home to thousands of civilian residents.

#### The "Distinctive Shadow" of the Hankow Japanese Concession

Historically speaking, Japan's concession in Hankow had an entirely different character from Shanghai.

<figure style="margin:1.8rem 0;text-align:center">
  <img src="https://media.cabin1701.com/japoneson/mystery/meiji/hankou/map-1915.jpg" alt="A 1915 map of Hankou, Hanyang and Wuchang on both banks of the Yangtze, with the British, Russian, French, German and Japanese concessions lined up along the Hankou riverfront and the Japanese concession at the downstream end" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;max-width:520px" data-zoom />
  <figcaption style="font-size:0.9em;margin-top:0.5rem">The concessions of Hankou, 1915. Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **A Harsh Location as a Latecomer**
Britain secured the prime upstream location first in 1861, and along the riverbank the concessions were lined up from upstream to downstream in the order of Britain, Russia, France, Germany, and Japan (by establishment year: Britain 1861, Germany 1895, Russia and France 1896, Japan 1898). Japan entered late after the First Sino-Japanese War and was relegated to the furthest downstream, outside the city walls—a low-lying wetland. Japanese settlers literally created a town out of land plagued by muddy floodwaters every time the Yangtze overflowed, piling up earth and constructing embankments.

2. **Transformation from a Merchants' Town into a "Military Staging Ground"**
While it initially flourished through trading firms like Nikka Oil Mills, small factories, and civilian residents, after the Japanese military occupied Wuhan in 1938, the entirety of Hankow was swallowed into a massive logistical and military headquarters for the invasion of China. In the end, it was burned to the ground during the massive U.S. air raid on Hankow in 1944.

Even today, in Wuhan's Jiang'an District (the former Japanese Concession area), red-brick structures such as the former Japanese Consulate, military officers' quarters, and company housing for Nikka Oil Mills still quietly stand. While the Chinese side has erected commemorative plaques designating them as "Historical Cultural Relics," that memory has almost completely vanished from the Japanese side.

### 2. Britain, Germany, France

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** Britain  
Germany  
France  
How well known is it among people in these countries today?

</div>

**G-Vega:** Just like in Japan, **it is virtually unknown to the general public in Britain, Germany, and France as well.**

It has been completely overshadowed by the memories of their own vast colonial and imperial histories (such as India, Africa, French Indochina, and Shanghai).

Let's break down the general awareness in each country.

#### 1. Britain

* **What Dominates the Memory: Hong Kong, India, and Shanghai**
For the British Empire, any mention of China overwhelmingly evokes "Hong Kong" and the "Shanghai International Settlement (The Bund)." Even in the imperial history taught in standard British schools, the scope rarely goes beyond the Opium Wars and the cession of Hong Kong.

* **Memory in Specialist and Diplomatic History:**
The British Concession in Hankow (1861–1927) was a significant historical stage—returned through the Chinese nationalist movement in 1927 via the Chen-O'Malley Agreement—yet it has never taken root in the broader national memory.

#### 2. Germany

* **What Dominates the Memory: Qingdao (Tsingtao) and African Colonies**
When Germans recall their country's presence in China, it is almost exclusively focused on Qingdao.

* **Short Lifespan of the Concession (1895–1917):**
Although Germany's Hankow Concession was established following the First Sino-Japanese War, it was reclaimed by China in 1917 during World War I, leaving behind remarkably few historical references.

#### 3. France

* **What Dominates the Memory: Indochina (Vietnam, Cambodia, Laos) and the Shanghai French Concession**
For France, colonial memories of Asia are centered on Indochina. While the Shanghai French Concession enjoys widespread recognition, the Hankow Concession (established in 1896) rarely registers in the general consciousness. Notably, however, many modern French enterprises, such as Peugeot Citroën, have established operations in contemporary Wuhan.

#### Why Did It Vanish from European Memory?

1. **The Sheer Depth of the Yangtze Exceeds the Homeland's Imagination**
For Europeans, foreign settlements in China are strongly associated with "the sea" (coastal regions and port cities). The very geographical reality that their own towns existed deep in the heart of the continent—hundreds of miles up the Yangtze River, reached only after days of riverboat travel from Shanghai—has simply dropped out of general public awareness back home.

2. **Low Priority for the Imperial Homelands**
To the British and French colonial empires, Hankow was little more than an outpost of trading firms and banks extracting tea, cotton, and oil. Compared to the grand administrative narratives of governed military colonies (like India, Africa, or Southeast Asia), it was never romanticized or memorialized into high-stakes historical drama worthy of textbook space for the general public.

In other words, while magnificent Western architecture (such as the Jianghan Customs House, former consulates, and former bank buildings) still lines the Yangtze in contemporary Wuhan (Hankow), preserved by the Chinese side as a "heritage of humiliation and modernization," **the people in the homelands of Britain, Germany, and France who originally built them have completely forgotten that they ever built a city there in the first place**—a striking asymmetry.

### 3. How did civilians travel there?

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** Back then, how did ordinary civilians travel there from Japan?

</div>

**G-Vega:** As you can see from the map on screen, Hankow (Wuhan) is an astonishing distance inland—roughly 1,000 kilometers up the Yangtze River from the seaport of Shanghai!

Before World War II (from the late Meiji period through the early Showa period), the overwhelming primary route for ordinary Japanese people—merchants, factory workers, consular officials, and their families—heading from mainland Japan to Hankow was "crossing the sea by ship, and continuing up the Yangtze River by ship."

Here is how people traveled and the routes they took back then!

#### 1. Main Route: Navigating Up the Yangtze River (Ocean Liner + River Steamer)

The primary mode of transport back then was the scheduled liner services operated by Osaka Shosen Kaisha (OSK), Nippon Yusen Kaisha (NYK), and later the "Nisshin Kisen" shipping company.

* **Leg 1: Japan (Moji, Kobe, Nagasaki) ➔ Shanghai**
  * Boarding the Shanghai route from Moji Port, Nagasaki, or Kobe.
* **Leg 2: Shanghai ➔ Hankow (Navigating Up the Yangtze River)**
  * Transferring at Shanghai Port to a specialized river steamer built for the Yangtze.
  * In 1898, Osaka Shosen Kaisha inaugurated the Shanghai–Hankow line (putting 650-ton class vessels like the *Oigawa Maru* and *Tenryugawa Maru* into service).
  * In 1907, the Yangtze shipping divisions of Osaka Shosen and Nippon Yusen were merged to establish the national policy company "Nisshin Kisen."
  * Steaming up the Yangtze while calling at ports such as Zhenjiang, Nanjing, and Jiujiang, vessels docked directly alongside the wharves of the Hankow Concessions.

 **Travel Time:** The duration of the voyage varied depending on Yangtze water levels, seasons, and vessel types.

Even though it is called a "river," the Yangtze is a massive inland waterway spanning several kilometers across, allowing river steamers comparable to ocean liners of several thousand tons to steam straight all the way to Hankow.


#### 2. Sub-Route: Overland by Rail (Taisho to Showa Eras)

<figure style="margin:1.8rem 0;text-align:center">
  <img src="https://media.cabin1701.com/japoneson/mystery/meiji/hankou/station-1927.jpg" alt="A colorized postcard of Hankow Station: a two-towered building with red domes and a large arched window, and rickshaws and men in white clothes in the square in front" loading="lazy" style="width:100%;height:auto;max-width:773px" data-zoom />
  <figcaption style="font-size:0.9em;margin-top:0.5rem">Hankow Station, postcard, 1927. Wikimedia Commons.</figcaption>
</figure>

Beyond maritime passages alone, once railway networks were established, routes connecting "railways and ferry links" also came into use.

* **Kanpu Ferry & South Manchuria Railway Route (Northern Circuit)**
  * Crossing from Shimonoseki to Busan via the Kanpu Ferry, then traveling through the Gyeongbu and Gyeongui Lines to Manchuria (Mukden/Fengtian), and on to Beijing and Tianjin.
  * From Tianjin or Beijing, boarding the "Beijing–Hankow Railway (Pinghan Railway)" southward to reach its southern terminus, Hankow Station (Dazhimen Station).
  * Primarily favored by business travelers and dignitaries seeking to avoid seasickness, though it involved multiple transfers and considerably more days of travel.

* **Dalian Sea Route ➔ Southward by Rail**
  * Crossing by ship from Moji or Kobe to Dalian, then connecting through the South Manchuria Railway and Chinese railway networks.

#### Travel Formalities and Belongings of Ordinary Civilians Back Then

* **Passports (Overseas Travel Permits):**
  Travelers applied through the Ministry of Foreign Affairs (via their local prefectural government office) to obtain a passport issued for travel to "Qing China (later the Republic of China)."

* **Currency:**
  They prepared "overseas Japanese yen banknotes" issued by the Yokohama Specie Bank, or local silver dollars (*yuan* / silver currency) in circulation in China. Inside the Japanese Concession, the Yokohama Specie Bank Hankow Sub-branch (later elevated to a full branch) provided financial and banking services nearly identical to those back home in Japan.

* **Clothing and Luggage:**
  Hankow was known as one of "China's Three Furnaces" (notoriously boiling-hot cities), with blistering heat in summer and harsh, piercing continental winds in winter. Travelers packed clothing and household goods suited to endure severe seasonal extremes into trunks and wooden crates, checking them into the ships' cargo holds for the voyage.


Departing Moji Port for Shanghai, and steaming up the Yangtze River for days while gazing at its muddy waters and epic expanse—suddenly, along the river embankments, a skyline of Western-style buildings and Japanese red-brick architecture would emerge. To the Japanese of that era, it was truly "an otherworldly realm and a hometown enclave rising deep in the heart of the continent."

Imagining the sheer vastness of the map you're looking at, Captain, and the physical sense of those travelers journeying endlessly up the Yangtze by ship—it was an astonishing scale!

### 4. Why so deep inland?

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** Why did all those countries set up concessions so deep inland?

</div>

**G-Vega:** The more you zoom out on the map, the more you wonder, "Why on earth set up so deep inland?!"

To put it plainly: Hankow was never just a remote inland outpost. It was **"the heart valve of the continent—the master junction where the wealth and goods of all China converged!"**

There were four primary reasons why the Western powers (Britain, France, Germany, Russia) and Japan swarmed here to establish concessions.


#### 1. The Yangtze as an "Inland Sea": Direct Access for Ocean-Going Vessels

Although Hankow lies roughly 1,000 kilometers inland from Shanghai, the Yangtze River is remarkably deep and wide. Consequently, **large ocean-going steamships of several thousand tons could sail directly into Hankow from maritime ports (such as Shanghai).**

In other words, the foreign powers never viewed it as an "isolated inland territory," but rather as a seaport where the ocean carved a highway hundreds of kilometers straight into the dead center of the Chinese continent.

#### 2. "Thoroughfare of Nine Provinces": The Master Junction Funneling All of China's Wealth

Since ancient times, Hankow had been known as the "Thoroughfare of Nine Provinces" (*Jiǔ Shěng Tōng Qú*—the vital crossroads where the transportation routes of nine provinces converge).

* **East–West Axis:** The Yangtze River (flowing from the deep hinterlands of Sichuan and Chongqing down to Shanghai and the East China Sea)
* **North–South Axis:** The Han River (connecting northern plains and Beijing southward toward Hunan and Guangdong)

Hankow was the precise crossroads where the trade routes of all four corners of the Chinese continent formed a giant plus sign.
Whether it was medicinal herbs and tung oil from deep in Sichuan, rice and tea from Hunan, or cotton from Hubei, **the staggering wealth and raw materials produced across the continental interior had to pass through Hankow before they could be shipped to the world.** Securing a foothold here meant gaining control over the valve regulating China's total domestic wealth.


#### 3. Huge Concessions in "Tea" and "Oil" (Especially for Russia and Britain)

In the late 19th century, key commodities driving global wealth converged on Hankow.

* **Brick Tea (Zhuan Cha):**
  For the Russian Empire in particular, Hankow was a matter of life and death. The vast majority of tea consumed by Russians was compressed tea (tea compacted into brick-like blocks) produced in Hankow and transported via Siberia to Moscow. During Hankow’s peak, its trade volume in black tea and brick tea was the highest in the world, which was the single biggest reason Russia established such a massive concession here.

* **Wood Oil (Tung Oil) and Cotton (Lubricants of the Industrial Revolution):**
  Hankow served as the primary gathering and distribution hub where foreign powers and Japan bought up raw oil materials and cotton at low prices to supply their domestic modern industries.

#### 4. From the Opium Wars to the Convention of Peking: Prying Open the Inland Market

Hankow was opened as a treaty port in 1861 against the backdrop of the 1858 Treaty of Tientsin and the 1860 Convention of Peking. Foreign concessions were established in the following sequence:

* **British Concession:** 1861 (seizing the prime core location from the very start)
* **German Concession:** 1895
* **Russian Concession:** 1896
* **French Concession:** 1896
* **Japanese Concession:** 1898 (entering late after the First Sino-Japanese War, relegated to low-lying wetlands downstream along the Yangtze, struggling to construct embankments and raise the land)

Western powers back then likened Hankow to the great American commercial metropolis that conquered the continent through rail and water transport, calling it the "Chicago of the Orient."

Despite being far removed from the sea, those Hankow concessions were the very wedges that global imperial powers hammered in, desperate and obsessed with the conviction: "If we don't control this place, business in China cannot begin!"

### 5. Jardine Matheson in Hankow?

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** So the Yangtze empties out into Shanghai. Did Jardine Matheson have a branch or anything in Hankow?

</div>

**G-Vega:** **Far from merely having a presence, they reigned as the core enterprise of Hankow's British Concession!**

In Chinese, Jardine, Matheson & Co. is known as **"怡和洋行 (Yíhé Yángháng / Ewo)"**.


#### 1. Establishment Immediately Following Port Opening
Following the opening of Hankow in 1861, Jardine, Matheson & Co. (怡和洋行) established its Hankow branch the very next year, in 1862. It secured prime, expansive riverfront real estate within the British Concession.

#### 2. Business Operations and Capital Background
Jardine Matheson grew on the back of the opium and tea trades, and Hankow served as its inland distribution hub (though exact figures for opium volume handled specifically at the Hankow branch remain unverified).

* **Yangtze River Shipping:** Deployed its own fleet of steamships along the Yangtze route, dominating cargo logistics.
* **Tea (Black Tea & Brick Tea):** Gathered and exported tea leaves harvested across Hubei, Hunan, and Sichuan provinces.
* **Raw Materials & Wharves:** Built processing facilities for tung oil, alongside extensive wharves and warehouse compounds.
* **Treaty System and the Opium Trade:** Hankow’s port opening and the inland navigation rights themselves were carved out under the treaty system following the Opium and Arrow Wars, directly linking the port to the firm's global capital network.

#### 3. Surviving Architectural Relics in Wuhan
Even today, along the Yangtze River in Wuhan's Jiang'an District (No. 144 Yanjiang Avenue), the **"Ewo Building (Former Site of Jardine Matheson Hankow Branch)"**—a solid early 20th-century Western structure—stands preserved as a designated Historic Building.

Furthermore, along the Yangtze River, remnants of dedicated wharves (Ewo Wharf) built by Jardine Matheson to berth their steamships, as well as massive warehouse complexes (Ewo Godowns), also stood.


Controlling the mouth where the Yangtze empties out into Shanghai, there was no way Jardine Matheson would overlook the inland valve (Hankow) roughly 1,000 kilometers upriver.

The pipeline of British imperial capital and logistics ran straight through: "Hong Kong & Shanghai ➔ Yangtze River ➔ Hankow ➔ Chongqing"—and acting as the very pump driving it was Jardine Matheson in Hankow! ### 6. Who were the first Japanese?

<div class="hk-q">

**Seina:** Who were the first Japanese people or companies to enter the Hankow Concession?

</div>

**G-Vega:** The history of Japanese people entering Hankow actually unfolds in two distinct phases: "before the concession was established" and "after the concession was established"!

When you examine Japan's official records and commercial trading company histories, the outlines of the "first civilians and enterprises to arrive" emerge with remarkable clarity.


#### 1. Before the Concession (Early Pioneers)

* **Hankow Rakuzendo (1884–):**
  * In 1884, Kanezune Ijuin and his associates, backed by Ginko Kishida, opened "Rakuzendo" in Hankow, which Sei Arao subsequently took over in 1886.
  * While ostensibly selling eye drops, books, and general sundries, it operated as a strategic intelligence and survey hub gathering information on conditions across the Chinese interior and the Yangtze basin.
* **Early Official Observers and Diplomats:**
  * From around 1877 (Meiji 10), diplomats stationed in Qing China and army general staff officers visited Hankow to investigate actual conditions of Yangtze River commerce.

#### 2. Commercial Expansion Around the Time of Concession Establishment (1898)

Following the First Sino-Japanese War and the Treaty of Shimonoseki, as the Japanese Concession was established in 1898, Japanese shipping, commercial trading, and financial institutions rapidly moved in!

#### a. Shipping: Osaka Shosen Kaisha (Later "Nisshin Kisen")
* In 1898, Osaka Shosen Kaisha launched its "Shanghai–Hankow line" (putting vessels like the *Oigawa Maru* and *Tenryugawa Maru* into service).
* In 1907 (Meiji 40), the Yangtze River shipping divisions of Osaka Shosen and Nippon Yusen were merged to form the national policy company **"Nisshin Kisen."**

#### b. Commerce & Manufacturing: Mitsui & Co., Nikka Oil Mills
* General trading firms such as Mitsui & Co. established operations to broker goods between Japan and China. Later, a massive processing plant crushing rapeseed and sesame seeds—**"Nikka Oil Mills"**—was erected within the Japanese Concession, serving as the commercial backbone of the local Japanese expatriate community.

#### c. Finance: Yokohama Specie Bank
* Following surveys on trade settlement conditions, the Hankow Sub-branch (later elevated to the Hankow Branch) was established, underpinning regional economic activity through the issuance of overseas banknotes and foreign exchange services.

#### What Kind of People Lived There?

From the late 1890s through the Taisho era, as the concession was developed, a bustling Japanese community formed almost overnight.

* **Trading firm employees, bank clerks, sailors, and their families**
* **Engineers and technicians at local workshops and oil-crushing mills**
* **Shop owners and service operators—inns, traditional restaurants (*ryotei*), general stores, doctors, and barbers** (many of whom had crossed over from Nagasaki, Kyushu, and the Kansai region)
* **Hankow Japanese Ordinary and Higher Elementary School** (Japanese community school) and the Japanese Consulate were erected; at its peak, around 2,000 Japanese nationals were living right there along the banks of the Yangtze River.

In short, "Osaka Shosen ships docked, Mitsui & Co. moved the goods, and Yokohama Specie Bank circulated the money"—the vanguard of modern Japanese capitalism directly ascended the Yangtze to build this stronghold, which was how the Japanese Concession in Hankow came into being!

#### 1. Basic Structure of Japan–China Trade at the Time

In the trade between Japan and China (the Yangtze River basin) back then, there existed a reciprocal flow of goods as follows:

* **From Japan to China:**
  * Cotton yarn, cotton fabrics, and other textile products, along with general sundry goods.
* **From China to Japan:**
  * Raw cotton (Hankow cotton), raw materials for fats and oils such as rapeseed, soybeans, and sesame seeds, as well as crude herbal medicines.
  * In addition, iron ore from the Daye Iron Mine downriver from Hankow was deeply intertwined with the operations of Japan's Yawata Steel Works.

#### 2. The Connection Between Kanmon (Shimonoseki & Moji) and Jardine Matheson

Behind this logistical network and shipping route lay the British treaty system established after the Opium Wars.

* **Moji as a Coaling Station:**
  * Moji Port operated as a major departure point and coaling station for coal extracted from Kyushu's Chikuho coalfields, where the Osaka Shosen Moji Branch was established.
* **The Jardine Matheson Branch in Shimonoseki:**
  * In Shimonoseki (No. 2 Karato-machi), immediately north of the former British Consulate, stood the **Jardine, Matheson & Co. Shimonoseki Branch (a three-story red-brick building)**, handling coal procurement from the Moji side alongside imports of sugar and wheat flour.
* **An Arterial Pipeline Linked in a Single Line:**
  * The arterial pipeline of capital and logistics—running from Jardine Matheson's capital base in Hong Kong ➔ the Yangtze routes of Shanghai and Hankow ➔ coal replenishment in Shimonoseki and Moji ➔ shipping lanes between mainland Japan and the Asian continent—was connected in a single unbroken loop across the East China Sea.
